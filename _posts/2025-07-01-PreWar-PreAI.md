In the world of radiation-sensitive science, not all steel is created equal. Low-background steel, metal forged before the first nuclear tests, remains prized for its purity, scavenged from shipwrecks and forgotten hulls. For most uses, modern steel is perfectly fine. But for ultra-sensitive instruments, even a whisper of contamination matters.

Dwight Schrute once said, "Whenever I'm about to do something, I think, 'Would an idiot do that?' And if they would, I do not do that thing". For scientists building equipment where even the faintest trace of radioactivity can ruin results, using modern steel would be that idiotic move. Instead, they seek out the rare, uncontaminated stuff.

### The Digital Parallel: Pre-AI Content

Now, imagine the digital equivalent. The rise of AI-generated content has flooded the internet with text that is, in many cases, indistinguishable from human writing. For most readers and most purposes, this isn’t a problem—AI content is often as credible and sometimes even clearer than its human counterpart. But for specialized use cases—legal judgments, medical advice, or high-stakes research—there’s a growing hunger for information that is demonstrably untainted by algorithmic hands.

Michael Scott once declared, "Would I rather be feared or loved? Easy. Both. I want people to be afraid of how much they love me". Authentic, pre-AI content may soon inspire a similar mix of reverence and nostalgia among researchers and decision-makers: valued for its credibility, sought after for its provenance.

### Authenticity in the Age of Abundance

Steel made after 1945 is still steel, and for almost every bridge, building, or bicycle, it works just fine. Likewise, the majority of digital content will continue to be useful, regardless of whether it was written by a person or a machine. But for those rare, high-impact scenarios—where trust, originality, and provenance are paramount—untainted, human-generated information may become a premium resource.

Andy Bernard once reflected, "I wish there was a way to know you’re in the good old days before you’ve actually left them". The internet’s “good old days” of wholly human content may be slipping into the past, becoming a resource whose value is only fully recognized in hindsight.

### The Temporal Catch: Information’s Expiry Date

Here’s where the analogy breaks down: while steel from 1910 is as strong today as it was a century ago, information ages. Even the most credible, pre-AI research fades as the world changes and new knowledge emerges. As Pam Beesly put it, "There’s a lot of beauty in ordinary things. Isn’t that kind of the point?" The beauty—and utility—of information is often fleeting, shaped by the context of its time.

### Conclusion: Provenance, Scarcity, and Moving Forward

Both low-background steel and pre-AI content remind us that authenticity and provenance can become precious in a world awash with abundance. But while steel’s value is fixed in its atomic purity, information is forever at the mercy of time, relevance, and the relentless churn of discovery.

Or, as Michael Scott once admitted, "Sometimes I'll start a sentence and I don't even know where it's going. I just hope I find it along the way". In the search for pure, credible knowledge, perhaps that’s the best any of us can do.
